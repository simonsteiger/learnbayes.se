Understanding how prior choices influence Bayesian inference is essential yet daunting at first.
Poor prior choices may indeed compromise analyses.

As Michael Betancourt explains in his [blog post](https://betanalpha.github.io/assets/case_studies/prior_modeling.html#2_Illicit_Elicitation) on Prior Modeling (sections 2 and 3), 
we do not have to know the precise effect of a treatment to specify a reasonable prior on its regression coefficient.
It is enough to understand which effects should be considered extreme (or even outright impossible).

<Aside type="caution" title="Frequentist priors">
By purely summarizing the data, frequentist methods implicitly place a uniform prior on all coefficients. This is ironically one of the poorer choices in many scenarios. 
Should we, a priori, really believe that an infinitely large effect is equally as likely as a near-null effect?
</Aside>

For example, it is not necessary to know if a treatment increases or decreases the relative risk of some adverse event occurring.
Despite our lack of knowledge, we may still be convinced that anything larger than a five-fold increase or decrease would be extreme.
